Most owners hear about the employer mandate secondhand: a "grace period," a penalty number from a friend, a quote from a carrier. The actual rules are more predictable than that. Once you understand them, the decision becomes a business decision instead of a deadline scramble. This guide walks through the rules in plain English, flags the things you need to be thinking about, then lays out the options side by side.

What "ACA" and "coverage" mean in this guide

Throughout this guide, "ACA" and "coverage" mean group medical coverage for employees. They don't mean dental, vision, or other benefits. (One narrow dental point, pediatric dental in small-group plans, gets a short note in section 5.)

01 — The threshold

Am I a large employer?

The law calls a business with 50 or more full-time employees, including full-time equivalents, an applicable large employer (ALE). ALEs are subject to the employer mandate and the annual IRS reporting that comes with it.[1]

The formula, step by step

  1. Count full-time employees each month. Full-time means an average of at least 30 hours of service a week, or 130 hours in a calendar month.[1] Paid time off (vacation, holiday, sick leave) counts as hours of service.[3]
  2. Convert part-time hours into full-time equivalents (FTEs). Add up the month's hours for everyone who isn't full-time, counting no more than 120 hours for any one person, then divide by 120.[1]
  3. Add the two for each month of the prior calendar year, total the 12 months, and divide by 12. Round a fractional result down.[1]
  4. 50 or more? You're an ALE for the current calendar year.
Monthly count = full-time employees + (part-time hours, capped at 120 per person, ÷ 120)
ALE status = average of last year's 12 monthly counts ≥ 50
Part-time hours matter only for this test. An ALE doesn't have to offer coverage to part-time employees, and a part-timer's subsidy can't trigger a penalty.[1]

!

At exactly 50, you can be both "large" and "small." Fifty is where the employer mandate starts: an average of 50 or more full-time employees, including full-time equivalents, makes you an ALE.[1] But Michigan insurance law still treats an employer as a small group up to an average of 50 full-time employees.[21] So a business averaging exactly 50 is subject to the mandate and still buys coverage in the small-group market, with small-group pricing rules (see section 5). At 51 and up, you're in the large-group market.

Commonly owned businesses count together

Companies with a common owner, or that are otherwise related under the controlled-group rules of Internal Revenue Code section 414, are generally combined to decide ALE status. If the group together reaches 50, every company in it is an ALE member, even one with only a handful of employees. Any penalty is still figured separately for each company.[1]

!

Worth checking early: if you or your partners own other businesses, or payroll runs through a separate LLC, have your CPA confirm how the ownership rules apply before you rely on a headcount.

Two narrow exceptions

  • Seasonal workers. You're not an ALE if you were over 50 for 120 days or fewer last year and everyone above 50 during that stretch was a seasonal worker (for example, holiday-only retail staff).[1] This rarely helps a year-round business.
  • Military coverage. An employee isn't counted toward the 50 for a month in which they have TRICARE or VA health coverage.[1]

A brand-new business with no prior-year history uses a different test, based on what it expects to employ in its first year.[1]

02 — The calendar

When does coverage have to start?

There is no 12-month grace period after you cross 50. ALE status is set once a year, for the calendar year, based on the prior calendar year's average.[1] The month you first hit 50 doesn't start a clock; the full-year average does.

The "12 months" people remember usually comes from one of two other rules: the look-back measurement period, used to decide which employees count as full-time (see section 8), or the first-year relief below. Neither delays ALE status itself.[2]

First-year relief: offer by April 1

In your first year as an ALE, if you offer coverage by April 1 to full-time employees who weren't offered coverage at any point in the prior year, you won't owe a penalty for January through March for those employees.[4]

  • Relief from the B penalty for those months applies only if the coverage offered by April 1 provides minimum value.[4]
  • Miss April 1 and penalties can reach back to January.[4]
  • It's a one-time rule. If you drop below 50 later and cross again, it doesn't apply a second time.[4]

03 — The exposure

The penalties: A and B

The IRS calls these "employer shared responsibility payments." There are two, and for any given month you can owe one or the other, never both. Neither applies unless at least one full-time employee enrolls in Marketplace coverage and receives a premium tax credit (a subsidy).[2]

The "A" penalty: not offering

Triggered when you don't offer minimum essential coverage to at least 95% of full-time employees (and their children under 26), and at least one full-time employee gets a subsidy.

Math: (all full-time employees − 30) × the annual amount, figured month by month at 1/12.[2][6]

"95%" means everyone but 5% of full-time employees, or everyone but 5 people if that's more.[2]

The "B" penalty: offering, but falling short

Triggered when you do offer coverage to 95% or more, but a full-time employee gets a subsidy because the offer was unaffordable, didn't provide minimum value, or they were among the up-to-5% not offered.

Math: each full-time employee who gets a subsidy × the annual amount (monthly at 1/12), capped at what the A penalty would have been.[2]

Amounts are indexed annually. Monthly figures are 1/12 of the annual amount, rounded to the cent.

Three details owners often miss
  • The first 30 full-time employees are subtracted. A business can be an ALE because of part-time hours and still have little or no A exposure if its full-time count sits near 30. In a group of related companies, the 30 is split among them in proportion to full-time headcount.[2][6]
  • The penalties aren't tax-deductible.[15] Premiums you pay generally are.
  • Being an ALE carries obligations even at $0. Tracking full-time status and filing Forms 1094-C and 1095-C apply regardless.[18]

Who can get a subsidy?

Generally, someone with household income between 100% and 400% of the federal poverty line who isn't eligible for affordable, minimum-value employer coverage or for Medicaid.[20] For a single person, 100% of the 2026 poverty line is $15,960.[12] A full-time hourly employee earning $17 an hour for 30 hours a week makes about $26,520 a year, roughly 166% of the poverty line for a one-person household. That is squarely in subsidy range, which is why B-penalty risk is higher for lower-wage workforces. Employees who enroll in Medicaid instead don't trigger a penalty.[2]

04 — The two tests

What "affordable" and "minimum value" mean

To avoid the B penalty for a full-time employee, the coverage you offer that person has to pass two tests: it must be affordable, and it must provide minimum value.[13]

Affordable

Coverage is affordable if the employee's cost for the lowest-cost employee-only (self-only) plan that provides minimum value doesn't exceed a set percentage of income.[2] That percentage is 9.96% for plan years beginning in 2026[10] and 10.22% for plan years beginning in 2027.[11]

✓

Only employee-only coverage is tested. You must offer coverage to employees' children under 26 to satisfy the A penalty, but the cost of dependent coverage doesn't have to pass the affordability test.[2][7] Spouses don't have to be offered coverage at all for this purpose.[2]

Because you don't know employees' household income, the IRS lets you use one of three safe harbors instead.[13][7]

Minimum value

A plan provides minimum value if it covers at least 60% of the total allowed cost of benefits expected under the plan[13] and includes substantial coverage of inpatient hospital and physician services.[14] Carriers and administrators typically confirm this with the HHS minimum value calculator.[13] A plan can have a high deductible and still provide minimum value. A plan built only around preventive care generally does not.

05 — The budget

How pricing works, and estimating your cost

Small group vs. large group

In Michigan's insured market, a small employer is one that averaged 1 to 50 full-time employees (including FTEs) during the preceding calendar year and has at least one employee on the first day of the plan year, with affiliated companies counted as one.[21] That matches the federal ACA definition, which counts employees with the same full-time-plus-FTE method used for the ALE test.[27] (The Michigan statute still contains an older "2 to 50 eligible employees" test, but the 1-to-50 definition has applied since January 1, 2018.[21]) At 51 and up, you're in the large-group market. As noted in section 1, a business at exactly 50 is already an ALE but still prices as a small group.

Small group (1–50)

ACA rating rules apply. Premiums can vary only by age (up to 3:1 for adults), tobacco use (up to 1.5:1), geographic rating area, and whether coverage is for an individual or a family.[22] Michigan uses the federal defaults.[23] Rates are built member by member ("age-rated"), and carriers may also offer composite (averaged) rates.[22]

Large group (51+)

The small-group rating limits don't apply.[22] Carriers typically price the group as a whole, looking at factors such as the age and gender mix, industry, and location, and, as groups get larger, claims history. Level-funded and self-funded options become more common.[24]

A short note on pediatric dental

Dental care for children, through at least the end of the month they turn 19, is one of the ten essential health benefits.[31][32] Non-grandfathered, fully insured small-group plans (1 to 50) must include those benefits, and that requirement falls on the carrier's plan, not on you as the employer.[29][30] The medical plan can leave pediatric dental out when the carrier is reasonably assured the children have it through an Exchange-certified stand-alone dental plan, which doesn't have to be bought on the Exchange.[33][34] Because that assurance is the carrier's job, the carrier may ask you to confirm that pediatric dental coverage is in place.[34]

Pediatric dental is not part of the employer mandate. A stand-alone dental plan is an excepted benefit, not minimum essential coverage, so it plays no role in the A or B penalty.[33][16][15] Nothing in these rules requires an employer contribution toward dental or applies a dental affordability test; the closest thing to a cost rule is the annual out-of-pocket cap inside Exchange-certified stand-alone dental plans.[29][35] Large-group and self-funded plans aren't subject to the essential-health-benefit requirement at all.[29]

Coverage tiers and how employers contribute

Plans are usually priced in tiers: employee only, employee + spouse, employee + child(ren), and family. The employer decides how much to contribute to each. Common approaches:

  • A percentage of employee-only. For example, 50% to 100% of the employee-only premium, with employees paying the full difference for dependents.
  • A flat dollar amount. A fixed monthly contribution the employee can apply to any tier. Predictable and easy to budget.
  • Tiered contributions. Different percentages or dollar amounts for each tier.

For context, the 2025 national average premium across all firm sizes was $9,325 a year for single coverage and $26,993 for family coverage ($9,211 and $26,054 at firms with 10–199 workers). Covered workers paid, on average, 16% of the single premium and 26% of the family premium (36% for family coverage at firms with 10–199 workers).[24]

!

Contribution strategy and affordability interact. Paying 50% of a $780 employee-only premium leaves the employee paying $390 a month. That's well above the 2027 FPL safe harbor ($135.92) and above the rate-of-pay limit for anyone earning less than about $29 an hour, so the offer would be unaffordable for many hourly employees and leave B exposure.

Participation: who will actually enroll?

Your cost depends on how many eligible employees enroll, not on how many you employ. Nationally, at firms that offer coverage, 80% of workers are eligible and 76% of eligible workers take it. At firms with a large share of lower-wage workers, only 43% of workers are covered by their employer's plan, compared with 64% at firms with a smaller share of lower-wage workers.[24] Younger, lower-paid, and shorter-tenure workforces tend to enroll at lower rates, and dependent enrollment is usually low when employees pay the full dependent cost.

06 — The philosophy

The strategy question

Before comparing plans, answer one question: do you want employees to jump on the plan, or do you want the best plan you can offer while limiting your cost exposure?

Two philosophies

Both are legitimate. The right answer depends on your margins, your labor market, and what you're trying to build.

"We want people on the plan"

  • Benefits become a recruiting and retention tool.
  • A richer plan and a bigger employer share drive enrollment.
  • Cost rises with every enrollee, so budget for high take-up.
  • Best when turnover is expensive or competitors already offer strong benefits.

"Compliant, good, and cost-contained"

  • Meet the mandate and offer something of real value.
  • Structure the offer so your maximum cost is known in advance.
  • Accept that fewer employees may enroll.
  • Best when margins are thin or much of the workforce is part-time or short-tenure.

The levers you control

  • Contribute on employee-only coverage. Affordability is tested only on employee-only coverage, so many employers fund that tier and let employees pay for dependents.[7]
  • Offer dependents without subsidizing them. Offering coverage to children under 26 is required for the A penalty; paying for it isn't.[2]
  • Use a waiting period. Up to 90 days for newly eligible employees.[17] In high-turnover businesses, this reduces enrollment among very short-tenure hires.
  • Choose your measurement method. For variable-hour staff, the look-back method fixes who counts as full-time for a stable period and smooths month-to-month swings (see section 8).[5]
  • Limit eligibility to full-time employees. You aren't required to offer coverage to part-time employees.[1]
  • Plan design. Deductibles, networks, and plan options change the premium. A plan that clears minimum value costs less than a rich one, and you can offer a richer buy-up alongside it.
  • Pick the right affordability safe harbor. FPL is simplest; rate of pay can allow a higher employee share for better-paid staff.[7]

Each option in detail

(a) Offer nothing and pay the A penalty

Some employers compare the A penalty with the cost of a plan and choose the penalty. It's predictable, but it isn't deductible,[15] it grows with every full-time hire above 30, it doesn't remove the reporting obligation,[18] and it leaves you without a benefit to recruit with.

(b) Minimum essential coverage (MEC) only

A MEC plan is a basic group health plan, typically built around preventive care, that counts as an offer of coverage. Offered to at least 95% of full-time employees and their children, it satisfies the A penalty.[2] Because it doesn't provide minimum value, any full-time employee can decline it, buy Marketplace coverage, and, if income-eligible, receive a subsidy. That triggers the B penalty for that person, capped at the A amount.[13] As section 3 shows, lower-wage full-time employees are more likely to qualify for subsidies, so B risk is higher for hourly workforces.[20]

(c) MEC plus an affordable minimum-value plan

Offer everyone a MEC plan and, alongside it, a minimum-value plan whose employee-only price meets an affordability safe harbor (often the FPL safe harbor). Full-time employees offered affordable, minimum-value coverage can't get a Marketplace subsidy,[20] so B exposure is largely taken off the table for them, and the offer itself handles the A penalty. Your cost is the employer share for employees who actually choose the MVP. Employees who want only basic coverage, or have coverage elsewhere, can take the MEC plan or waive. For many cost-conscious ALEs, this combination handles compliance while tying the employer's cost to actual enrollment.

(d) A full comprehensive plan

A traditional medical plan, often with a choice of options, and a meaningful employer contribution. As long as the lowest-cost employee-only option that provides minimum value is affordable, it satisfies both penalties.[2] It's the strongest recruiting tool, and the largest and least predictable cost.

(e) Individual coverage HRA (ICHRA)

Instead of a group plan, the employer gives employees a tax-free monthly allowance to buy their own individual coverage. It can satisfy the mandate, but only if the allowance is big enough where your employees live.[25]

ICHRA detailHow it counts as an offer, and the affordability test

Under IRS proposed regulations that employers may rely on until final rules take effect, an ICHRA counts as an offer of coverage for the A penalty.[25] Employees, and any family members the ICHRA covers, have to be enrolled in individual health insurance (or Medicare) to use it, and you can't offer the same class of employees both an ICHRA and a traditional group plan.[38][18] To avoid the B penalty, the ICHRA must be affordable. Take the monthly premium for the lowest-cost silver plan for self-only coverage on the Exchange for that employee, subtract the monthly ICHRA amount, and the difference can't be more than the affordability percentage of income: 9.96% for plan years beginning in 2026.[39][10] An ICHRA that passes that test is also treated as providing minimum value.[39] For the penalty, you can price that silver plan by the employee's primary worksite instead of home address, and use the same income safe harbors as a group plan.[25][18] CMS publishes a lookup table to help with the calculation.[26] ICHRAs offer budget control but depend heavily on individual-market prices where your employees live.

08 — The paperwork

Measurement for variable-hour workforces, and reporting

Once you're an ALE, you need a consistent way to decide which employees are full-time and must be offered coverage. There are two methods: count hours every month, or use a look-back period that locks in status for a stretch of months.[2][5]

Keep in mind: the look-back method only decides who must be offered coverage and how penalties are figured. It is not used to decide whether you're an ALE.[2] For a team with many people near 30 hours a week, look-back measurement usually makes full-time status, and your cost, far more predictable.

Every ALE also files Forms 1094-C and 1095-C each year, even if it owes no penalty, and the IRS uses those forms to propose penalties.[18][19] Getting measurement and reporting right is mostly an administrative job, but it's where avoidable penalties come from. The details are below.

Measurement methodsMonthly vs. look-back, and the periods involved

Monthly measurement

Count each employee's hours every month. 130 or more hours means full-time for that month.

Simple, but someone hovering around 30 hours a week can move in and out of full-time status, which makes offers and payroll deductions hard to manage.

Look-back measurement

Measure hours over a standard measurement period of 3–12 months. Employees who average 30+ hours a week are treated as full-time for the following stability period, which must be at least 6 months and no shorter than the measurement period. An optional administrative period of up to 90 days can sit between them for enrollment.[5]

New variable-hour, part-time, and seasonal hires can be measured over an initial measurement period of 3–12 months.[5]

Filing detail: Forms 1094-C and 1095-CWhat they are, deadlines, electronic filing
  • Form 1095-C goes to each full-time employee and to the IRS, showing what coverage was offered each month. Form 1094-C is the summary transmittal to the IRS.[18]
  • Deadlines for calendar-year 2026 forms, due in early 2027: furnish 1095-Cs to full-time employees by early March (the January 31 date gets an automatic 30-day extension), and file with the IRS by February 28 on paper or March 31 electronically.[37][18] A deadline that lands on a weekend or legal holiday moves to the next business day,[18] and February 28, 2027 is a Sunday, so the paper date slides into early March too. The IRS posts exact dates in each year's instructions, so check them when they come out.
  • Furnishing on request. You don't have to send every full-time employee a 1095-C. Under the Paperwork Burden Reduction Act (December 2024), you can instead post a clear, easy-to-find notice on your website by the furnishing deadline saying employees can ask for a copy, keep it up through October 15, and send any requested form within 30 days of the request. The notice needs an email address, a mailing address for requests, and a phone number.[36][18]
  • Your first forms cover your first ALE year and are due early the following year. ALE status comes from the prior year's average, so if your 2025 average reached 50, 2026 is your first ALE year and these early-2027 forms are your first. If your 2026 average is the first to reach 50, you're an ALE starting in 2027 and your first forms are due in early 2028.[1][18]
  • Electronic filing is required if you file 10 or more information returns of any kind during the year, counted in the aggregate (W-2s count), so most ALEs must e-file.[18][28]
Letter 226-JHow the IRS proposes a penalty

The IRS uses these forms, along with employees' subsidy information, to identify potential penalties, and proposes a penalty by sending Letter 226-J, which you can respond to.[19] Accurate forms are your first line of defense.

09 — Next steps

Owner's checklist, and why an advisor matters

If you're near or past 50, pull these together. They're the inputs that decide your timing, your exposure, and your best strategy.

  • Monthly headcount and hours for every employee, full- and part-time, going back to January of last year.[1]
  • Any related businesses under common ownership, which may need to be counted together.[1]
  • Your first ALE year, and whether you offered coverage in the prior year (this determines first-year relief).[4]
  • Which employees sit near 30 hours a week, and whether their hours swing month to month.
  • Your philosophy: maximize enrollment, or compliant, good, and cost-contained?
  • A strategy from section 7, or a combination of them.
  • Contribution levels and an affordability safe harbor that work together.[7]
  • A measurement method and waiting period (up to 90 days).[5][17]
  • How employees will enroll: online or in person, and in which languages.
  • Who will prepare and file Forms 1094-C and 1095-C each year.[18]
